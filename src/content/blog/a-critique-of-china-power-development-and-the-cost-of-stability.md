# **A Critique of CN: Power, Development, and the Cost of Stability**

Criticizing China does not mean denying the achievements of Chinese society, the Chinese people, or the country’s extraordinary economic development. A meaningful critique should instead examine China as a political, economic, and social system—and focus on its structural contradictions, governance costs, and long-term risks.

China has demonstrated remarkable state capacity. It can mobilize resources on an enormous scale, build infrastructure rapidly, coordinate industrial policy, and pursue long-term national strategies. But the same institutional characteristics that create these strengths can also generate serious weaknesses.

The central question is therefore not whether China is simply “good” or “bad.” It is whether a system designed for rapid development and strong coordination can adapt to an increasingly complex society.

## 1. Concentrated Power and the Problem of Error Correction

One of the most significant structural risks in China is the high concentration of political and administrative power.

Centralized decision-making has obvious advantages. It can reduce coordination costs, mobilize resources quickly, and allow the government to pursue projects that would be difficult to implement in a fragmented political system.

However, the same structure creates another problem:

> The more concentrated the decision-making process, the greater the potential consequences when major decisions are wrong.

Every political system makes mistakes. The critical question is not whether mistakes occur, but whether the system can identify, discuss, and correct them quickly.

In a highly hierarchical administrative structure, lower-level officials may have incentives to emphasize positive outcomes, avoid reporting negative information, and align themselves closely with higher-level political priorities. As information moves upward through multiple bureaucratic layers, reality can become filtered.

This creates a potential **feedback problem**.

A government may possess enormous executive capacity, but executive capacity alone does not guarantee good decision-making. Strong implementation must be matched by equally strong mechanisms for criticism, feedback, and institutional correction.

Otherwise, the system risks becoming highly efficient at implementing the wrong decision.

## 2. When “Stability” Becomes More Important Than Solving Problems

Social stability is understandably important for a country as large, populous, and economically diverse as China.

However, an excessive emphasis on stability can create a problematic governance incentive:

> It may sometimes become easier to suppress the visibility of a problem than to solve the problem itself.

Local governments may be particularly sensitive to:

* public protests;
* online controversy;
* media attention;
* negative publicity;
* incidents that threaten local political stability.

As a result, administrative attention can shift from asking:

**“Why did this problem occur?”**

to:

**“How can we prevent this problem from becoming visible?”**

From the perspective of systems theory, this is dangerous.

Healthy systems depend on feedback:

**Input → Feedback → Adjustment.**

If negative feedback is excessively restricted, the system may appear stable while internal problems continue to accumulate.

Silence is not always evidence of stability.

Sometimes, it simply means that the mechanisms for expressing dissatisfaction have become weaker.

## 3. Bureaucracy and the Problem of Formalism

One of the most visible problems in China's governance system is bureaucratic formalism.

A policy may begin with a reasonable objective. But as it moves through multiple levels of administration—from the central government to provinces, cities, counties, districts, and基层 institutions—the original objective can gradually be transformed into administrative procedures.

The result may be:

* forms;
* reports;
* photographs;
* checklists;
* inspections;
* meetings;
* presentations;
* documentation.

The purpose of work can gradually shift from **solving a problem** to **demonstrating that work has been done**.

This is a classic problem of bureaucratic incentive structures.

When officials are evaluated according to measurable indicators, institutions naturally optimize for those indicators.

If the evaluation focuses on:

* the number of meetings;
* the number of visits;
* the number of reports;
* the amount of documentation;

then the system will produce more meetings, visits, reports, and documentation.

But these metrics do not necessarily measure whether the underlying social problem has actually improved.

The result can be a paradox:

> Everyone is busy, but the system is not necessarily becoming more effective.

## 4. The Real Estate Model Created Growth—and Dependency

For decades, real estate played a central role in China's economic development.

But real estate was never just about housing.

It became connected to:

* local government finance;
* land sales;
* banking and credit;
* construction;
* steel and cement;
* household wealth;
* urbanization.

This created a deeply interconnected economic structure:

**Local governments sell land → developers acquire land → banks provide credit → households purchase housing → property values remain high → land finance continues.**

The problem is that once a single sector becomes responsible for too many functions, the entire economy becomes dependent on its continued expansion.

When the property market slows, the consequences spread through multiple parts of the economy:

* local government revenue declines;
* developers face financial pressure;
* banks face increased risks;
* household wealth weakens;
* consumer confidence declines.

This is why China's real estate problem cannot simply be reduced to the question of whether housing prices should rise or fall.

It is fundamentally a **structural dependency problem**.

## 5. Local Government Debt and the Fiscal Structure

Another major challenge is the relationship between central and local governments.

Local governments are responsible for a large share of public services and development tasks, including:

* infrastructure;
* education;
* healthcare;
* urban development;
* social services;
* investment promotion.

However, local fiscal capacity does not always fully match these responsibilities.

For years, many local governments relied heavily on:

* land revenue;
* local government financing vehicles;
* debt-funded investment.

This model was easier to sustain during periods of rapid economic growth and expanding real estate markets.

But as land revenue declines and economic growth slows, the fiscal pressure becomes increasingly visible.

The structural problem can become cyclical:

> Revenue declines → borrowing increases → debt expands → financial pressure grows → future fiscal flexibility decreases.

China's long-term economic transition will therefore require more than simply stimulating growth.

It will require a deeper restructuring of the relationship between **local responsibilities, fiscal revenue, debt, and public services**.

## 6. An Education System Defined by Excessive Competition

China does not suffer from a lack of respect for education.

In some ways, the opposite is true.

Education has become one of the most important mechanisms for social mobility, and because access to high-quality opportunities is highly competitive, the educational system often becomes a continuous process of selection:

Primary school → Middle school → High school → Gaokao → Graduate school → Civil service exams → Employment.

The Gaokao itself has an important egalitarian function. Compared with systems dominated by family connections or inherited privilege, standardized examinations provide millions of people with a relatively transparent path toward social mobility.

But the competition has also become excessive.

The result includes:

* credential inflation;
* intense pressure to attend graduate school;
* the expansion of civil service examination culture;
* youth unemployment anxiety;
* delayed entry into the workforce.

The deeper problem is that education can become extremely effective at **ranking people**, without being equally effective at **developing diverse forms of talent**.

Students may become highly skilled at:

* standardized tests;
* memorization;
* following structured requirements;
* identifying the “correct” answer.

But the labor market does not necessarily need an unlimited supply of people with identical educational credentials.

## 7. Healthcare: The Problem Is Not Only Resources, but Distribution

China has made substantial progress in expanding healthcare access. However, high-quality medical resources remain heavily concentrated in major cities and large tertiary hospitals.

This creates a familiar pattern:

Patients often travel long distances to seek treatment in major hospitals, even for conditions that could theoretically be managed within a stronger primary healthcare system.

The result is:

* overcrowded major hospitals;
* overworked doctors;
* uneven regional access;
* underutilized or undertrusted基层 medical institutions.

The problem is not simply that patients “do not understand” hierarchical medical treatment.

Patients behave rationally according to their level of trust.

If primary healthcare institutions cannot consistently provide reliable diagnosis and treatment, patients will naturally seek care at major hospitals.

Therefore, the solution cannot simply be:

> “Do not go to large hospitals.”

The more fundamental solution is:

> **Give patients a reason to trust primary healthcare institutions.**

Without improving the quality and credibility of基层 healthcare, administrative attempts to redirect patients will have limited effectiveness.

## 8. The Demographic Challenge

China's demographic problem may be more difficult than previous population debates suggested.

For decades, China was concerned about having too many people.

Now, the concern has shifted toward:

* declining birth rates;
* population aging;
* a shrinking workforce.

The low fertility rate cannot simply be explained by the idea that young people have “changed their attitudes.”

Raising children involves substantial costs:

* housing;
* education;
* healthcare;
* childcare;
* time;
* career risks, particularly for women.

Therefore, encouraging childbirth through slogans alone is unlikely to solve the problem.

The real question for young families is more practical:

> Who will pay for raising children?
> Who will take care of them?
> Who will bear the career consequences?

China's demographic challenge is therefore inseparable from broader issues involving:

**housing, employment, education, childcare, social security, and gender roles.**

## 9. Limited Space for Criticism Weakens Society's Ability to Diagnose Itself

Perhaps the most fundamental issue is the relationship between political criticism and national identity.

A healthy society should be capable of allowing people to:

* criticize policies;
* investigate failures;
* express disagreement;
* discuss institutional problems.

Criticism is not necessarily a threat to a political system.

It can function as a form of **social diagnosis**.

However, in China, criticism of a policy, criticism of the government, criticism of the Communist Party, and criticism of the country can sometimes become politically or emotionally conflated.

This creates a dangerous chain of reasoning:

**Criticizing a policy → opposing the government → opposing the country → being “anti-China.”**

But these categories should not automatically be treated as identical.

A government is not the same thing as a country.

A political party is not the same thing as a civilization.

And criticizing a policy is not necessarily equivalent to rejecting one's nation.

The real danger is not that people criticize a society.

The real danger is that:

> **Everyone knows that a problem exists, but nobody can openly discuss it.**

When that happens, the system gradually loses access to accurate information about itself.

# The Central Contradiction

If China's greatest strength is its extraordinary capacity for coordination and implementation, then one of its greatest long-term challenges may be ensuring that this power is matched by equally effective mechanisms for:

* transparency;
* feedback;
* institutional accountability;
* public participation;
* error correction.

China has already demonstrated that a highly coordinated state can produce extraordinary economic transformation.

The more difficult question is what happens next.

As China enters an era defined by:

* slower economic growth;
* demographic aging;
* real estate restructuring;
* local government debt;
* rising expectations for public services;
* increasingly complex social interests;

the challenge may no longer be rapid development.

It may be **governing complexity**.

The future of China may depend not only on how effectively the state can act, but also on how effectively society can provide the state with accurate feedback.

> **A powerful system does not become stronger merely by increasing its capacity to act. It also becomes stronger by improving its capacity to recognize when it is wrong.**

In that sense, China's most important long-term challenge may not be a lack of state power.

It may be whether the mechanisms that restrain, inform, and correct that power can develop at the same pace as the power itself.
